The one rule

One million dollars in hundreds is a stack 3 ft 7 in tall, roughly the height of a six-year-old. Every height on this page follows from that.

1 · Street level

One hundred buildings

Line up all 132 million households from poorest to richest and cut them into 100 equal slices of 1.32 million households each. Every building holds the same number of people by construction; what changes from one to the next is the money. Each building is the average stack in its slice, drawn to one linear scale. Pick how tall the chart is allowed to be. Whatever you pick, the last building will not fit.

2 · Inside the tallest building

The 1% is not one building

The 100th building is an average of 1.32 million households, and the average lies. Open it and it splits into ten slices of 0.1%. Open the tallest slice again, and again. Each floor down, the same shape reappears: nine buildings and one tower that does not fit. Five levels down, the tower has a name.

3 · The climb

Scroll up. Keep going.

Twelve stacks stand on one sidewalk: the bottom half's average, the median household, then the average household in each exclusive band on the way up: 80th to 85th percentile, 85th to 90th, 90th to 95th, 95th to 98th, 98th to 99th, 99th to 99.9th, 99.9th to 99.99th, 99.99th to 99.999th, the top 0.001%, and Elon Musk. Exclusive matters: the 80th to 85th stack is the average of those 6.6 million households only, not dragged upward by anyone above them. The bands narrow as they climb because the curve steepens: each percentile step near the top is a bigger dollar step than the one before. The scale is always linear; only the ceiling rises as you scroll. Real landmarks pass at their real altitudes. Watch which stacks are still visible when you reach space.

Nobody reaches the Moon. Not Musk, not the top 1% together, not the whole country.

The Moon is 239,000 miles away. In $100 bills that is $352 trillion, twice the net worth of every American household combined. Musk's stack stops near 610 miles up, above the space station and the Starlink shell. The median household's stack is a sheet of paper on the ground beside it, about four million times shorter.

The irony the original video landed on still holds, and it is sharper now: the average one-percenter, the 12-story building everyone got angry at, stands closer to the family with nothing than to the man in orbit.

4 · Headcount

How many households are in each band

Percentiles hide headcount by design: every percentile holds exactly 1.32 million households. Dollar bands answer the other question. Here the x-axis is money and the height is people. The bands are unequal on purpose, so every bar is labelled with its range, and the top bands are so small they get their count written above them.

5 · Wealth vs income

Two ladders, one scale

Income is what arrives in a year. Wealth is what has piled up: past income, plus returns on it, plus what was inherited. At the middle, a household's wealth is about two or three years of its income. At the top, the ratio explodes, because returns compound and paychecks do not. The whole income ladder fits under the Eiffel Tower. The wealth ladder leaves the frame.

6 · Your stack

7 · What we think

Ideal, guessed, actual

In 2011 Michael Norton and Dan Ariely asked more than 5,000 Americans how wealth was split between five equal groups of households, and how it should be. Nine in ten chose the first skyline. Almost everyone guessed the second. The third is what the Federal Reserve measured in the first quarter of 2026.

Each figure shows the share of all household wealth held by a fifth of households, poorest on the left. The row beneath converts that share into the average stack a household in that fifth would hold if today's $174 trillion were divided that way.

Sources and method

Where the numbers come from

Two kinds of numbers appear on this page: dollar totals and averages for groups of households, and entry thresholds for individual percentiles. They come from different sources and are labelled separately wherever both are shown.

  7. Landmark heights and orbital altitudes are standard published figures. $100 bills are 0.0043 in thick, so 10,000 of them, $1 million, stack 43 inches: 3 ft 7 in.

Method

  • Group totals are anchored to the Fed. The five Fed groups sum to $174.0 trillion across 132 million households. Survey-based averages are scaled by a factor that varies smoothly across percentiles and is solved so each Fed group matches its published total exactly, with no step at the group boundaries.
  • Two kinds of dollars, kept apart. The survey undercounts wealth relative to the Fed's macro totals: below the 99th percentile the Fed counts roughly 17 to 70 percent more per household, most of it in the middle, where pension entitlements and under-reported assets are concentrated. Every building, its average and its dollar range are in Fed dollars, so the average always sits inside the range. The "find your building" lookup, the headcount bands and the two ladders use survey dollars, because that is how a person measures their own net worth, and each says so.
  • Inside the top 1%, the curve is bent, not scaled. There the Fed totals sit slightly below what the survey and World Inequality Database thresholds imply, so the thresholds are kept and the curve between them is bent until each bracket's total matches. The Fed stops at the top 0.1% ($25.1 trillion); its split into the top 0.01% ($14 trillion), top 0.001% ($10 trillion) and top 132 households ($6.1 trillion) follows the World Inequality Database's shape and the Forbes 400, and should be read as approximate.
  • Headcount bands are survey-based. The net-worth histogram counts households between survey thresholds, interpolated between published percentiles. Above $1 billion it uses about 1,300 households, consistent with the Forbes count of 959 individual billionaires plus near-billionaire households.
  • The climb uses exclusive bands. Each stack in the climb is the average of the households inside that percentile range only. Nested "top X%" averages are dragged upward by everyone above; the 100 buildings and the drill-down still show them where the label says "Top".
  • People versus households. Counts are households; the people figure multiplies by 2.58, the 2026 average household size.
  • Everything is linear, and in feet. No chart on this page uses a log scale; what changes is only the ceiling. Heights are shown in inches below a foot, feet up to 150,000 ft, and miles beyond that.
